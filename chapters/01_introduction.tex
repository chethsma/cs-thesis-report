\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}


\section{background}
\label{sec:background}

Occupant monitoring systems are becoming an increasingly important component of vehicle safety as vehicle interiors, 
occupant behaviour, and the role of the driver evolve with higher levels of driving automation. Conventional 
occupant-safety systems were primarily developed around relatively well-defined seating configurations and 
nominal upright postures. In more highly automated vehicles, however, occupants may adopt reclined, rotated, 
or otherwise non-nominal seating postures that differ from these conventional assumptions \cite{mcmurry2018oop}. 
Automation also enables a broader range of non-driving-related activities, which can alter occupant posture and, 
in conditionally automated vehicles, influence the driver's readiness to resume control \cite{zhao2024takeover}. 
Consequently, occupant monitoring is evolving from simple seat-occupancy detection toward a more detailed description 
of occupant presence, body characteristics, and seating posture.

This transition is also reflected in current safety regulations and consumer-assessment frameworks. In the United States, 
FMVSS No.~208 introduced advanced-airbag requirements intended to reduce airbag-induced injury to vulnerable occupants
while maintaining frontal-crash protection \cite{fmvss208}. For conditionally automated driving, UN Regulation No.~157 
requires a Driver Availability Recognition System to determine whether the driver is present and available to resume 
control \cite{uneceR157}, while European regulations additionally require advanced driver-distraction warning functions 
\cite{eu2023addw}. Euro NCAP's 2026 assessment framework extends occupant monitoring beyond the driver by introducing 
dedicated Occupant Monitoring requirements, occupant-stature classification, automatic passenger-airbag disabling, and 
additional credit for systems capable of optimising restraint and airbag performance for different occupant physiques 
\cite{euroncap2026}.

The relevance of occupant-state information is expected to increase further as automated-driving systems progress toward 
vehicles that can operate without conventional driver controls. In June 2026, UNECE adopted the first global regulatory 
framework for fully automated driving systems. Existing occupant-protection regulations, however, 
do not yet fully cover all seating configurations that future automated interiors may enable. NHTSA's occupant-protection 
rule for automated vehicles without conventional driving controls is limited to vehicles with conventional forward-facing 
seating, while rear-facing and ``campfire'' seating configurations remain areas requiring further investigation. A UNECE 
review similarly concluded that side-facing or rear-facing seats and torso recline angles greater than $25^{\circ}$ would 
require substantial changes to existing occupant-protection regulations. These developments indicate that future 
vehicle-safety systems will require increasingly accurate information about who occupies a seat and how that occupant is 
positioned.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec}
The need for richer occupant-state information is primarily safety-driven because occupant posture and physical characteristics 
influence the interaction between the human body and restraint systems. Real-world crash analysis using GIDAS data showed that 
belted reclined occupants had higher odds of MAIS~2+, MAIS~3+, and MAIS~4+ injury than occupants seated upright 
\cite{schaefer2021reclined}. In conditionally automated driving, non-driving postures can additionally increase takeover 
time and reduce takeover quality \cite{zhao2024takeover}. Reliable estimation of seated posture is therefore relevant both 
before a crash, where it can indicate driver readiness, and during a crash, where occupant geometry directly affects restraint 
interaction.

Occupant size or weight provides another relevant input because the appropriate restraint response is not identical for all 
occupants. Advanced-airbag systems can use information about occupant weight, size, and seating position to suppress deployment 
or adapt deployment intensity when necessary \cite{nhtsaAdvancedAirbag}. NHTSA notes that, when occupant weight or size is available, 
different inflation levels may be selected for smaller and larger adults \cite{nhtsaAdvancedAirbag}. Multi-stage airbag systems 
support such adaptation by independently controlling inflation stages to better match restraint force to occupant characteristics, 
as described in NHTSA guidance on advanced-airbag functionality \cite{nhtsaAdvancedAirbag}. This is particularly important because inappropriate or overly aggressive airbag deployment has 
historically caused serious and fatal injuries, especially for small occupants and occupants positioned close to the airbag module 
\cite{nhtsaAirbags,nhtsaAirbagFatalities}. Since an airbag must also be replaced after deployment \cite{nhtsaAirbags}, suppressing 
deployment when it is not required can provide an additional economic benefit.

Obtaining occupancy, posture, and occupant-size information reliably from a single sensing modality remains difficult. Camera-based 
monitoring provides rich semantic and geometric information, including body appearance, body landmarks, and upper-body posture, but
its performance can deteriorate because of poor illumination, glare, shadows, or occlusion \cite{salazar2025dms}. Seat-pressure sensing 
is independent of visible-light conditions and directly measures body-seat contact and load distribution, making it suitable for 
occupant detection and classification \cite{reed2001ocatd}. Its limitation is different: pressure measurements contain comparatively 
little semantic information, and similar contact distributions can be produced by different occupants or postures.

The two sensing modalities therefore provide complementary information rather than redundant measurements. RGB images primarily 
describe the visible geometry and semantic state of the occupant, whereas seat-pressure measurements describe the physical interaction 
between the occupant and the seat. Multimodal fusion offers a means of combining these complementary observations and can potentially 
provide more reliable predictions when one modality becomes uncertain or degraded \cite{ortega2022multimodal}. Based on this premise, 
this thesis investigates whether feature-level fusion of monocular RGB cabin images and seat-pressure distribution maps improves the 
classification of seat occupancy, seated posture, and occupant size or weight class compared with equivalent RGB-only and pressure-only 
models.





\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Reliable occupant monitoring requires the estimation of multiple occupant states, including seat occupancy, 
occupant category, posture, and occupant weight class . This requirement is becoming increasingly relevant as 
vehicle-safety assessment frameworks such as Euro NCAP place greater emphasis on occupant monitoring and the 
adaptation of safety functions to occupant characteristics \cite{euroncap2026}. Reliably estimating these states 
from a single sensing modality, however, remains challenging.

Camera-based systems provide rich semantic and geometric information for detecting occupants and estimating body posture, 
but their performance can deteriorate under poor illumination, glare, shadows, and partial occlusion \cite{salazar2025dms}. 
Seat-pressure sensing avoids these visual limitations and can capture body-seat contact, load distribution, and posture-related 
pressure patterns. Even relatively low-resolution pressure configurations have demonstrated useful posture-recognition capability
\cite{zhao2021lowrespressure}. Nevertheless, recent pressure-based studies show that performance can degrade when seating 
conditions change and that geometrically similar postures may produce ambiguous pressure patterns \cite{wang2025pressure}. 
Thus, the two sensing modalities provide complementary occupant information while exhibiting different failure modes.

Multimodal in-cabin monitoring has already demonstrated that heterogeneous signals such as video, seat pressure, and 
physiological measurements can be combined successfully for specific tasks such as driver takeover-state recognition
\cite{quan2024ergonomicfusion,teshima2024multimodal,xueetal2019heteromultimodal}. However, existing studies provide limited evidence on whether 
combining camera images and seat-pressure maps improves a unified set of occupant-monitoring tasks—particularly occupancy, 
occupant classification, posture, and occupant mass classification—relative to equivalent unimodal baselines.

The central problem addressed in this thesis is therefore to determine whether feature-level fusion of monocular RGB and seat-pressure 
information provides a measurable improvement in occupant-state estimation compared with camera-only and pressure-only models. 
The comparison must be conducted using synchronized data, equivalent task definitions, and consistent evaluation criteria so that
any performance gain can be attributed to multimodal fusion rather than differences in data or evaluation conditions.





\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}


"To what extent does feature-level fusion of synchronized RGB cabin images and low-resolution 
seat-pressure distribution maps improve the classification of seat occupancy, seated posture, and 
occupant size class compared with equivalent RGB-only and pressure-only models."

\begin{enumerate}
    \item RQ1.1: How do the RGB-only and pressure-only models compare in classification performance across seat occupancy, 
    seated posture, and occupant weight classification?
    \item RQ1.2: To what extent does the fused RGB–pressure model improve classification performance over the best-performing
         unimodal baseline for each classification task?
    \item RQ1.3: What contribution do sensor weighted combination(WC) and cross-sensor correlation(CS) fusion framework does to the 
        classification performance of the RGB–pressure fusion model?
    \item RQ1.4: How robust is RGB–pressure fusion to controlled degradation of either sensing modality compared with the
         corresponding unimodal models?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

This thesis investigates supervised, per-seat occupant-state classification using two heterogeneous sensing modalities: 
monocular RGB cabin images and low-resolution seat-pressure distribution maps. The study is restricted to adult occupants 
and considers three target attributes: seat occupancy, seated posture, and occupant mass class. RGB-only and pressure-only 
models are evaluated as unimodal baselines and compared with a feature-level RGB--pressure fusion model under the same dataset 
partitions and evaluation criteria.

Seat occupancy is formulated using the classes \textit{occupied} and \textit{empty}. For occupied seats, posture is classified 
as \textit{upright}, \textit{forward lean}, \textit{reclined}, or \textit{side lean}. Occupant mass is represented using three 
study-specific classes: \textit{light} (49--$<63.35$,kg), \textit{medium} (63.35--$<89.45$,kg), and \textit{heavy} (89.45--101.2,kg). 
The detailed geometric definitions of the posture classes and the justification of the mass-class boundaries are presented in 
the experimental methodology.

The pressure modality is represented using synthetic low-resolution pressure maps generated to reproduce the spatial 
configuration of the target pressure-sensor system. Consequently, the conclusions regarding pressure-based and multimodal 
performance are limited to this simulated representation. Transfer to a physical pressure mat and quantitative validation 
of the simulated pressure distributions against real measurements are outside the scope of this study.

Children, occupants outside the defined adult mass range, postures outside the predefined classes, production-vehicle deployment, 
direct restraint-system control, and validation under crash conditions are not considered. The objective is limited to 
evaluating whether RGB--pressure fusion improves the classification performance and robustness of the defined occupant-state 
tasks relative to the corresponding unimodal models.
